## 1. Why now
- **Demand is large and growing.**
  - Autism: 1 in 31 US 8-year-olds (CDC 2025).
  - ADHD: 11.4% of US 3–17-year-olds, about a third of them untreated.
  - Dyslexia: 5–20%.
  - About 7.5M US students are served under IDEA.
  - EHC plans in England are growing about 11% a year.
  - Specialist shortages (SLPs, special-ed teachers) mean families wait months.
- **The market is fragmented and has little evidence behind it.** AAC is the only clear category. Most other products are built on sound methods but have not themselves been tested. Parents keep reporting the same problems:
  - billing traps (Speech Blubs, Joon)
  - hours of setup and sync bugs (Goally)
  - children losing interest within weeks (Joon)
  - $250–$300 AAC apps that Medicaid rarely funds on a consumer tablet
  - infantilizing design for older learners
  - over-stimulation even in "autism apps"
- **Positioning is open.** ABA-branded apps sell to some parents but alienate autistic adults and many SLPs and OTs. The market has no scaled brand that affirms neurodiversity. Tiimo (2025 iPhone App of the Year) shows that design co-created with neurodivergent people can win mainstream.
- **Funding channels are opening.** ESA programs give larger awards for disability (for example, Florida FES-UA). IDEA money is relatively protected from the ESSER cliff. UK EHCPs are increasing.

## 2. Guiding stance: neurodiversity-affirming, by design
- **Goals the learner and family choose:** communication (any modality), self-advocacy, regulation, independence, learning and joy.
- **We never** target stimming, eye contact, "looking normal" or compliance. We never make cure or recovery claims, and never use puzzle-piece imagery or functioning labels.
- **Identity language** is the user's choice (identity-first or person-first).
- **Paid autistic, ADHD and dyslexic co-designers** on every app. A **named neurodivergent advisory board** with real authority over content, alongside SLP, OT, BCBA and special-education advisors.
- **Compatible with IEP/EHCP goals.** We produce progress data professionals can use, without building "compliance trackers".
- **Honest evidence.** Every claim is mapped to an evidence tier. We pre-register pilots and publish results. We make education claims, not treatment claims, unless we deliberately take an FDA route.

### App cards

#### 1. Wavelength Day (2–17)
- **Problem:** Transitions and unpredictability cause distress. Families build visual schedules by hand with laminated cards. Existing apps are dated, iOS-only, or take hours to set up.
- **Experience:** A pictorial day strip (Now/Next/Done), first–then boards, a waiting board, calm countdown warnings ("2 more minutes, then shoes"), and printable cards for screen-free use. Older learners get calendar-style views that look like a regular planner.
- **AI role:** Drafts a schedule from a sentence ("school day with swimming on Tuesdays"). Drafts **social narratives** for new events (dentist, first day), which a caregiver must review and edit before the child sees them. All AI drafts use a human-authored style guide.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** Real photos or symbols. Calm Sensory Dial. Visual, audio and haptic cues. Switch- and AAC-compatible.
- **Riskiest assumption:** AI drafting cuts setup to ≤5 minutes while keeping caregivers' trust.
- **Validation test:** Timed setup tasks with 20 caregivers comparing a Figma prototype to Choiceworks, plus a trust rating of AI-drafted narratives.

#### 2. Wavelength Voice (2+)
- **Problem:** Robust AAC costs $150–$300. Families are overwhelmed by choosing a system and fear relearning if they switch. Tablet AAC rarely gets Medicaid funding.
- **Experience:**
  - A **core-vocabulary** AAC with **consistent motor planning**: words never move.
  - Grid size grows with the user.
  - Natural child and teen voices, multilingual.
  - Guided onboarding with an SLP: we provide the setup tools and partner with SLPs rather than replacing them.
  - Families and SLPs can share and edit a common vocabulary set.
  - Open Board Format import and export, so users are not locked in.
- **AI role:**
  - Optional phrase expansion ("water + cold + please" → "Can I have cold water, please?") that **shows exactly what it added**. The user confirms before anything is spoken, keeping authorship.
  - Context-aware word suggestions.
  - **Never** speaks unprompted.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** Switch scanning, eye gaze, keyguard layouts, adjustable dwell time, high contrast, and quiet or visual-only feedback options.
- **Pricing hypothesis:** An affordable subscription (≈$9–12/mo) *or* a one-time price well below incumbents. Free for supporter accounts. IEP- and school-fundable.
- **Riskiest assumption:** SLPs will trust and recommend a new AAC system, and AI expansion is accepted by the AAC community.
- **Validation test:**
  - An SLP advisory panel review (n=10).
  - AAC-user co-design sessions, including adult AAC users.
  - A Wizard-of-Oz phrase-expansion test that measures authorship satisfaction.

#### 3. Calm Harbor (4–17)
- **Problem:** Sensory overload and dysregulation. Most "calm" apps flash or overload, and some regulation tools use techniques that risk photosensitive users.
- **Experience:**
  - A sensory profile builder, done with an OT or caregiver ("I like: deep pressure, humming; I don't like: sudden noise").
  - Body check-ins by **self-report** (pictorial "engine" meter).
  - A calm corner with slow, non-flashing visuals, breathing with haptics, and favorite sounds.
  - Co-regulation scripts for adults.
  - Sensory-diet planning.
- **AI role:** Suggests strategies from the profile and past self-reports. **No emotion recognition from face or voice**: that is banned in EU education and ethically fraught.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** WCAG 2.3.1-safe (no flashing). Sound optional. Screen-dim mode. Works offline.
- **Riskiest assumption:** Children will use it *during* dysregulation, not only in calm moments.
- **Validation test:** OT-led in-clinic sessions with a paper and Figma calm corner (n=15). Caregiver diary study.

#### 4. ReadWave (5–14)
- **Problem:** Dyslexic learners need structured, explicit, cumulative phonics. Existing tools are cartoonish (Nessy) or expensive, and reading apps punish slow reading.
- **Experience:** A structured-literacy scope and sequence (phonemic awareness → decoding → fluency → spelling). Multisensory **access** (see, hear, say, tap, trace). Age-respectful decodable texts, including mature themes for teens. Read-along help.
- **AI role:** Read-along speech recognition **tolerant of speech differences**, with a tap fallback. Adaptive review. Generates decodable practice texts **within** a controlled word list, reviewed by humans.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** BDA typography, background tint, adjustable spacing, TTS everywhere, no timers.
- **Evidence posture:** Market as "built on structured literacy". The multisensory component is not presented as the active ingredient (Stevens 2021 meta-analysis). Pre-register a pilot.
- **Riskiest assumption:** Schools and ESA families will buy it alongside Orton-Gillingham tutoring they already pay for.
- **Validation test:** Interviews with dyslexia tutors and SENCOs (n=15), plus a 4-week paper-based concierge pilot measuring fluency probes.

#### 5. Focus Crew (7–17)
- **Problem:** ADHD executive-function struggles (starting tasks, time blindness). Reward apps wear off in weeks (Joon), and planners need too much adult admin.
- **Experience:**
  - "Break it down" task steps.
  - Visual time timers.
  - **Body-doubling** rooms with peers or a calm virtual co-worker (clearly a tool, not a friend).
  - Movement breaks.
  - A reward system with a **built-in fading plan** agreed with the child.
  - Gentle progress with pause days.
- **AI role:** Task breakdown (Goblin Tools-style) that the child edits. Time estimates that learn from the child's history.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** Low-distraction UI, visual and haptic reminders, and a choice of voice or text.
- **Riskiest assumption:** Engagement lasts beyond 4 weeks without escalating rewards.
- **Validation test:** A 6-week diary study (n=20 families) comparing a paper Focus Crew with a fading plan against the current approach. Track engagement curves.

#### 6. Social Compass (8–17)
- **Problem:** Social-skills programs often teach masking. Autistic youth need tools for understanding others **and** for being understood (the double-empathy problem), plus self-advocacy.
- **Experience:**
  - Scenario explorations that compare perspectives both ways.
  - Scripts for self-advocacy ("I need a break", "Can you say that more directly?").
  - A **"my profile" card** the teen writes to share with teachers and peers.
  - Group-chat etiquette.
  - Online safety.
- **AI role:** Scenario role-play (clearly labeled simulation), reflection prompts. Content is co-written with autistic adults.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** Text-first with optional voice, no forced eye contact or face tasks, and mature themes.
- **Riskiest assumption:** Autistic teens find it affirming rather than corrective, and parents still value it.
- **Validation test:** Co-design workshops with autistic teens and adults (n=12). Rate "affirming vs. masking" on the prototype scenarios.

#### 7. Spark Switch (2–17, higher support needs)
- **Problem:** Children with higher support needs (profound and multiple learning disabilities, CP, intellectual disability) are barely served. Most apps assume touch dexterity.
- **Experience:** Switch- and eye-gaze-first multisensory activities: press, and lights, music and haptics respond. Cause-and-effect progressions. Choice-making ("music or bubbles?"). Turn-taking games with a caregiver. Fine-motor play for children who can touch.
- **AI role:** Minimal. It adapts the timing and dwell of scanning from response patterns, and suggests next activities to the caregiver or teacher.
- **Accessibility & sensory:** Works with 1–2 switches, eye gaze and head tracking. Adjustable scan speed. Sensory intensity controlled by the caregiver. Compatible with Cosmo-style tangible switches.
- **Riskiest assumption:** Special schools and OTs will buy it (hardware compatibility, procurement).
- **Validation test:** Sessions at 2 special schools using existing switches with Figma-driven prototypes (Wizard-of-Oz). Teacher value ratings.

## 6. Business model and go-to-market
- **Families (B2C/ESA):**
  - The Wavelength Family plan (≈$19/mo or $149/yr, covering siblings) includes all apps.
  - Wavelength Voice is also sold separately at an affordable AAC price.
  - ESA-reimbursable, with a Florida FES-UA priority.
  - Free trial with a reminder before conversion. One-tap cancel.
- **Schools and districts (B2B):** Special-education licences funded by IDEA ($10–40 per student per year). IEP progress monitoring. VPAT/ACR, DPA, Clever/ClassLink.
- **Clinicians:** SLP, OT and BCBA practice licences for between-session "homework". Clinician-authored content marketplace (later).
- **UK:** Special schools, local authorities (EHCP provision), DSA for older learners.
- **Channels:**
  - parent advocacy communities and neurodivergent creators
  - special-ed directors
  - clinicians as influencers
  - ESA vendor lists
  - disability organizations, as partners and not only as marketing
- **Moat:**
  - co-designed, affirming content and trust with the community
  - longitudinal progress data mapped to IEP/EHCP goals (switching costs)
  - accessibility excellence (switch and eye gaze)
  - an open-format, low-lock-in AAC, which builds loyalty

## 7. Ethics, safety and compliance
- **Children's data is highly sensitive** (behavioral, voice, AAC logs). COPPA 2025 applies: separate consent for any sharing or training (off by default), on-device processing where feasible, a strict retention schedule, and a learner/family data-control center.
- **HIPAA** where clinical partners share PHI. FERPA for schools.
- **EU AI Act:** no emotion recognition. Avoid "exploiting vulnerabilities of disability". Human oversight of any adaptive placement.
- **FDA/FTC:** no diagnosis or treatment claims. Each claim is tied to an evidence tier in a public claims register.
- **Autonomy:** Learner agency first. Teens see and control what is shared. Adult AAC users own their vocabulary data.

## 8. Risks and mitigations
| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Community backlash (ABA framing, "fix the child") | An ND advisory board with veto power over content. Affirming language. Transparent co-design |
| AAC trust barrier (SLP gatekeepers) | SLP advisory panel. Open formats. No-lock-in guarantee. Evidence partnerships |
| Long B2B cycles; Medicaid cuts (OBBBA) | ESA and B2C first. District special-education second. No reliance on Medicaid DTx |
| Novelty decay | Plan reward fading. Rely on intrinsic motivation and real-life utility (Day, Voice) |
| Over-claiming efficacy | Claims register. Pre-registered pilots. University partner |
| Breadth across 7 apps | One shared hub and design system. Sequence the MVP: Day + Voice + Calm Harbor first |

## 9. Discovery and validation focus
- **Top assumptions:**
  1. Caregivers will adopt one integrated hub if setup takes ≤5 minutes.
  2. SLPs and AAC users will accept AI phrase expansion that keeps authorship.
  3. Affirming framing is compatible with what parents are willing to pay for.
  4. ESA and IDEA channels will reimburse.
  5. Calm-by-default design measurably improves comfort and persistence.
- **North-star:** *Weekly moments of self-directed communication, regulation or independence per learner*, reported by the learner or caregiver. Guardrails: Sensory Comfort Rating ≥4/5, zero billing complaints, ND advisory-board sign-off on all content.
- **Three-year ambition:**
  - Year 1: Wavelength Day + Voice + Calm Harbor MVP. ESA approval in 3 states. A pre-registered pilot with a university or clinic.
  - Year 2: ReadWave, Focus Crew, district special-education pilots.
  - Year 3: Social Compass, Spark Switch, UK expansion, published outcomes.
